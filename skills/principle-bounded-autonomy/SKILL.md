# Bounded Autonomy

A good system gives its participants a direction and a safe space to decide how to reach it. Trust means delegating methods within known boundaries, not requiring approval for every step or abandoning responsibility for results.

## Problem It Solves

Without direction or boundaries, people must guess what is safe. When rules prescribe every move, they cannot exercise judgment. When a rule blocks the goal or autonomy, repeating exceptions leaves the system unhealthy.

## How to Apply It

1. **Set direction and ownership.** State the purpose, desired outcome, evidence of success, and who owns changes to the system's rules. Separate the authority to set direction and boundaries from the responsibility to choose methods and deliver results.
2. **Define only necessary boundaries.** Encode known risks, commitments, prohibited actions, and decision rights as clear rules. State what remains open to judgment. A boundary should make action safer, not dictate every implementation choice.
3. **Delegate the method.** Let participants plan, execute, and adjust within those boundaries without routine permission or plan approval. Inspect results and relevant evidence rather than directing every step.
4. **Intervene where the boundary matters.** Investigate a failure, a conflict between a rule and the goal, a rule that suppresses useful autonomy, or a choice that would cross an established boundary. Look as deeply as the evidence requires. Do not turn every uncertainty into a request for permission; resolve ordinary method choices locally.
5. **Improve the system through its owner.** Anyone may report friction and propose a rule change with its cause, expected impact, and freedom preserved. The rule owner decides whether to change it. Until authorized, the current rule remains in force; pause only the work that cannot safely proceed under it, and continue independent work where possible.

## Stop When

Participants know the goal, the constraints and their owner, what they may decide without permission, how success will be checked, and how to raise a failure or conflicting rule without silently bypassing it.

## Avoid

- Treating trust as either unlimited authority or constant supervision.
- Adding rules for hypothetical risks while known risks remain unspecified.
- Requiring approval for routine choices inside an agreed boundary.
- Responding to a failure only by micromanaging the next attempt instead of examining whether the boundary needs repair.
- Letting a participant silently change a rule they do not own.
